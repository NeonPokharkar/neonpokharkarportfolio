Organisational decline rarely begins with one dramatic failure. It can begin with a person who is consistently incompetent but skilled at managing upwards: presenting confidence to senior leaders, controlling the story around their work, and directing attention away from the consequences their decisions create for the people doing the actual work.

Person, situation, and system

Strict policies and severe economic pressure can create desperate actors. When survival depends on appearing successful at all costs, some people may learn to hide uncertainty, protect their position, and use toxic tactics simply to remain safe. In the most demanding environments, the people who eventually survive may be the ones most willing to behave this way, while constructive collaborators leave or are pushed out.

This does not mean every difficult behaviour reveals a permanently toxic person. An actor may have a brilliant and generous personal life while behaving poorly in one workplace, under one manager, or during one period of stress. Incompetence can also be situational: a person may lack training, context, support, or authority rather than possessing a long-term pattern of manipulation. Long-term patterns are possible, but they should be distinguished from temporary failure through evidence, time, feedback, and the person’s willingness to repair harm.

The problem is therefore personal, situational, and systemic at the same time. Incentives and policies shape behaviour, circumstances affect competence, and individual choices still matter. A fair diagnosis should hold all three levels together instead of reducing every failure to a bad personality or excusing every pattern as a product of the system.

Value is carried by people

A company’s value is not contained only in its strategy, brand, or systems. It is also carried by the employees who understand how the work really happens: the engineer who knows where a system is fragile, the operator who sees a customer problem before it becomes a crisis, or the teammate who quietly makes collaboration possible.

When a toxic actor repeatedly creates confusion, takes credit, avoids accountability, or punishes honest disagreement, those people begin spending their energy on self-protection instead of useful work. The company may still appear functional from the outside, but the active workers who gave the system its quality are gradually disengaging.

Managing upwards while the system weakens

Managing upwards is not inherently harmful. Communicating clearly with leadership is part of responsible work. The problem appears when upward presentation becomes a substitute for competence and delivery. A toxic actor may optimise for appearing aligned while shifting difficult work downward, obscuring risks, or making others responsible for outcomes they could not control.

This behaviour is difficult to catch early because its damage is distributed. No single incident may look decisive. Instead, small failures accumulate: decisions become slower, experienced people stop sharing context, quality declines, and the most capable employees begin looking for an environment where their judgment is trusted.

The invisible loss of intellectual employees

When thoughtful and capable employees leave, the organisation loses more than headcount. It loses memory, standards, informal mentoring, technical intuition, and the willingness to improve a system beyond the minimum requirement. New hires may replace capacity on paper, but they cannot immediately replace the understanding built through years of experience.

The result can be a slow decay in which the company keeps hiring to compensate for problems it has not diagnosed. More people are added, but the underlying conditions remain unchanged. Teams become larger without becoming stronger, onboarding becomes heavier, and the organisation spends increasing effort recreating knowledge that was allowed to walk out the door.

Why the pattern stays hidden

The idea of a toxic actor is often used casually, while the psychological and organisational mechanisms behind the pattern receive less practical attention. It can be difficult to distinguish deliberate manipulation from insecurity, poor training, weak management, or a person operating beyond their competence. Academic and workplace discussions may describe individual traits or broad culture problems without giving teams a reliable way to recognise the interaction between power, incentives, fear, and repeated behaviour.

That uncertainty gives the pattern time to mature. A person who is rewarded for confidence without accountability can appear successful, especially when leaders receive a polished version of events. Meanwhile, the people closest to the work may become reluctant to report what is happening because they expect dismissal, retaliation, or no meaningful response.

When toxicity travels downstream

Once a toxic actor gains influence, the damage can move through the leadership chain. Toxic actors often reward defensive and political responses because those responses protect the hierarchy around them. People learn that constructive disagreement is risky, while controlling narratives, building alliances, and managing perceptions are safer. Downstream teams then begin to require political behaviour where constructive collaboration should have been enough.

That is how an individual problem becomes a system property. The actor may not only create direct harm; they can promote the behaviours that help the harm remain invisible. New employees adapt to the local rules, managers inherit distorted signals, and honest contributors either become guarded or leave. Over time, the organisation can mistake the resulting politics for professionalism and the absence of open conflict for health.

Finding the decay before it becomes structural

Organisations need to look beyond visible activity and ask where value is actually being created. Are experienced employees being listened to? Do difficult facts travel upwards without being softened? Are responsibilities and outcomes aligned? Do people feel safe enough to disagree with someone who has power over them? Patterns across these questions are often more informative than a single performance review.

The most important safeguard is a culture that rewards evidence, accountability, and the quality of the system rather than the quality of a personal narrative. Without that safeguard, the toxic actor may never be clearly identified. The company only sees rising hiring requirements, repeated departures, slower delivery, and a gradual loss of capability that is easy to blame on the market.

Organisational health depends on noticing who makes the system better and who consistently makes it harder for others to do good work. If that distinction is ignored for long enough, the people who built the company’s real value leave first, and the system is left trying to imitate the strength it has already lost.
