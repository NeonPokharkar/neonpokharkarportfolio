A business does not sustain itself simply because its service is valuable. It sustains itself when it can deliver that value at a cost lower than what the value is worth to the customer and the market. The distance between the value created and the cost of creating it is where the business earns the room to survive, improve, and grow.

Value is not the same as cost

When someone pays for a service, they are not paying for the exact amount it costs the business to provide it. They are paying for the outcome, convenience, reliability, speed, or transformation that the service creates for them. A business can therefore charge according to the value experienced while working continuously to reduce the resources required to produce that experience.

This is the foundation of many sustainable business models. The service may remain familiar to the customer, while the machinery behind it becomes more efficient: better processes, stronger software, improved infrastructure, clearer operations, and more capable teams can all reduce the cost of delivering the same or better outcome.

Optimisation creates the margin

The margin is not only a financial metric. It is the practical space that allows a business to absorb uncertainty, invest in quality, respond to competition, and continue serving customers through changing conditions. If the cost of delivery rises until it matches the value of the service, the business loses that space.

Optimisation can happen in many forms. A product can automate repetitive work, use infrastructure more efficiently, reduce errors, shorten delivery cycles, or design a simpler path for customers and employees. Each improvement means that fewer resources are needed to preserve the same value. Over time, small reductions compound into a business that is more resilient without necessarily making the service feel smaller.

The responsibility of reducing cost

Cost reduction is not automatically good. Cutting support, weakening reliability, underpaying people, or degrading the customer experience may lower costs briefly while destroying the value that made the service worth paying for. Sustainable optimisation is different: it removes waste and friction without removing the substance of the promise.

The best businesses ask a careful question: what can be made more efficient without making the experience less valuable? That question keeps efficiency connected to purpose. It treats technology, operations, and design as ways to preserve quality while using fewer resources, rather than as excuses to shift costs onto customers, workers, or society.

Leadership through changing margins

A successful leader must keep looking for ways to optimise cost as the market adapts. When competitors enter, prices shift, and new profit margins become the norm, yesterday’s operating model may no longer be enough. The response should not be panic or a race to compromise the service. It should be a disciplined search for better processes, wiser prioritisation, and new ways to deliver the same essential value with less waste.

That optimisation must remain ethical. A leader has to distinguish between removing inefficiency and removing responsibility. The goal is to build a business that can compete while continuing to respect its customers, employees, partners, and the society around it. Efficiency becomes sustainable when it strengthens the system rather than quietly transferring its costs to people with less power.

Leadership also requires a degree of detachment. Losses can be painful, but reacting to them emotionally can make a difficult situation worse. A clear leader is able to acknowledge a loss without becoming consumed by it, understand what the numbers and circumstances are saying, and decide what needs to change. This does not mean becoming indifferent; it means creating enough distance to think well while the business moves through uncertainty.

Markets can feel like whirlwinds: margins tighten, assumptions break, and several pressures arrive at once. The leader’s work is to stay grounded enough to separate signal from noise, take a necessary loss without panic, and keep making decisions that protect the long-term ability of the business to create value.

Sustaining the same value, better

A durable business keeps widening the gap between the value it creates and the cost of creating it. It does this not by charging endlessly more for the same service, but by learning how to provide that service with greater clarity, efficiency, and care.

In that sense, profitability is a signal of disciplined value creation. The business earns because it has learned to organise its systems so that the outcome remains meaningful while the effort required to produce it steadily improves. Sustainability comes from maintaining that balance: protect the value, reduce the unnecessary cost, and reinvest the difference into making the system stronger.
